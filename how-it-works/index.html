How a Hydraulic Ram Pump Works

The four-stage cycle, from the original patents

A hydraulic ram pump moves water uphill using no electricity, no fuel and no motor. It runs on the one thing it already has: water falling a short distance. It wastes most of that water to lift a little of it a long way, and it will do that continuously for years with no moving part other than two valves.

The mechanism is over two centuries old and every patent describing it has long since expired, so the design belongs to everybody. What follows is drawn from three of them — US4328A, US281749A, US755467A — the newest of which ran out of term in 1921.

The four-stage cycle

  1. 1

    The water gets moving

    Water runs down the drive pipe under nothing but its own fall and escapes through the open waste valve. Nothing is being pumped yet. All that happens in this stage is that a long column of water picks up speed.

  2. 2

    The waste valve slams

    Once the water is moving fast enough, the flow itself drags the waste valve shut. The valve does not close because of a timer or a spring setting -- it closes because the water it is passing pulls it closed.

  3. 3

    The shock does the lifting

    A moving column of water has real momentum, and stopping it suddenly has to put that energy somewhere. The pressure spikes far above what the fall alone could ever produce, and that spike is what shoves the check valve open and pushes water up the delivery pipe. This is the whole trick of the machine.

  4. 4

    Recoil, and around again

    Pressure drops, the check valve shuts so nothing falls back, the waste valve drops open, and the cycle restarts -- typically around once a second, all day, with no power and no attention. The air chamber's trapped air is what turns those separate shocks into a steady trickle out of the delivery pipe.

The counter-intuitive part

Most people assume the pump is powered by the weight of the falling water. It is not. It is powered by the shock of stopping it. That is why a ram needs a long drive pipe rather than a tall one — the pipe's job is to give a heavy column of water somewhere to build up speed before the waste valve stops it dead.

Where each idea came from

These are not decoration. Each patent below contributed a specific piece of the machine that is still in every ram built today.

US4328A — Hydraulic ram

Benjamin S. Benson, Harford County, Maryland · Dated December 26, 1845 (antedated August 5, 1845)

Two-source arrangement: stream water is the motive power while spring water is the fluid actually lifted. Also a lever-and-bucket arrangement that regulates flow automatically by responding to water level, so the pump keeps running unattended.

“the raising of spring water from a lower to a higher level by the motive power of river, or branch water”

Expired - Lifetime · term ran out 1862-12-26

US281749A — Hydraulic ram

George Yellott, Towson, Maryland · Published July 24, 1883

States the momentum principle plainly -- the moving column in the drive pipe behaves as a solid mass, which is why stopping it abruptly produces the pressure spike that does the lifting. Adds a piston-valve that moves automatically inside a cylindrical outlet pipe, and a supplemental air chamber whose compressed air cushions the shock and re-establishes outflow for the next stroke.

“water moving through the drive-pipe...acts like a solid body”

Expired - Lifetime · term ran out 1900-07-24

US755467A — Hydraulic ram

George H. Earle Jr. (Bryn Mawr, PA) and George B. Shainline (Wayne, PA) · Filed December 10, 1903; granted March 22, 1904

Incoming water strikes an adjustable bucket carried on a pivoted lever, and that closes the valve at the outlet; the bucket is adjustable specifically to compensate for variations in pressure. The closure traps the water's momentum and delivers force to a piston in a working cylinder. Modern experimental work still finds waste-valve geometry to be a dominant efficiency variable.

Expired - Lifetime · term ran out 1921-03-22

What “expired” actually means here

A US patent is a time-limited deal: the inventor publishes exactly how the thing works, and in exchange gets a limited monopoly on building it. When the term runs out the monopoly ends but the published instructions stay published — permanently, in full, free to read.

US utility patents filed on/after 1995-06-08 run 20 years from the earliest filing date. Patents filed before that ran 17 years from grant, or 20 from filing, whichever was longer. Every patent in this file predates 1995 and Google Patents independently prints `Expired - Lifetime` on each, so both tests agree.

Separate from patent term: MOST US works PUBLISHED in 1930 or earlier are in the US public domain by copyright age as of 2026-01-01 (95 years), assuming any required copyright formalities were met. That is why the FIGURES in the pre-1931 patents below can be treated as free to reproduce. A patent expiring is about the right to BUILD the machine; publication date is about the right to COPY the drawing. Two different clocks -- do not conflate them.

Practical upshot: the three patents above stopped constraining anybody more than a century ago. Nobody needs a licence from them, and there is nobody left to ask.

Said precisely, because the precise version is the useful one: an expired patent's claims stop binding people, but that is not a clearance opinion on whatever you personally go and build. A modern pump with modern refinements could still read on some other patent that is still in force. What is settled is that these designs, as published in these documents, are nobody's property any more.

The parts, by name

  • Drive pipe — the long feed from the source. Where the water builds momentum. Rigid, never a soft hose.
  • Waste valve — the one that slams shut. Does the work of stopping the column, and spills the water the pump does not lift.
  • Delivery check valve — one-way gate into the air chamber. Opens on the pressure spike, shuts so nothing runs back.
  • Air chamber — a sealed pocket of trapped air. Absorbs each shock and pushes back between beats, converting hammer blows into steady flow.
  • Delivery pipe — the climb to the tank.

Deliberately not listed: pipe diameters, drive-pipe length ratios and parts costs. Several rules of thumb circulate for these and none was traced to a primary source while writing this page, so none is printed. A number with no source is exactly what makes the scanned PDFs already on this topic hard to trust.

The arithmetic

How much water actually arrives is set by four numbers and one efficiency figure:

q = eta * Q * H / h   q = delivered flow Q = supply flow into the drive pipe H = drive head (the fall from source down to the pump) h = delivery head (the lift from the pump up to the tank)

That rearranges to the D'Aubuisson efficiency, eta = (q * h) / (Q * H). D'Aubuisson measures both heads from the ram itself. The other convention in the literature, Rankine, credits only the NET lift above the supply surface -- it treats the water as raised through (lift minus fall) rather than the full lift -- and so always returns a lower number for the same pump. Always say which one you mean. The calculator on this site uses D'Aubuisson, matching the formula already implemented in app.js.

Careful: the textbooks swap these two letters

TEXTBOOKS SWAP THESE TWO LETTERS AND IT IS A REAL TRAP. This site uses H = drive head (the fall down to the pump) and h = delivery head (the lift up to the tank), giving eta = q*h/(Q*H). Many references invert them -- H = the lift, h = the supply head -- and write the identical physical law as eta = q*H/(Q*h). Both say the same thing: efficiency = (delivered flow x lift) / (supply flow x fall). Read the symbol definitions before trusting any ram formula, including this one.

The D'Aubuisson formula assumes ideal valve timing and zero friction. Reported real-world losses: drive-pipe friction 5-8 percentage points, waste-valve closure timing 3-7 points, air-chamber damping 2-5 points. Rankine's efficiency is always less than D'Aubuisson's for the same pump, because it credits only the net lift above the supply level.

For scale: Published experimental work on 3-inch rams reports a peak D'Aubuisson efficiency of 67.66% at a waste-valve diameter of 2.75 in and delivery-valve diameter of 2.2 in. (Experimental Study of Waste Valves and Delivery Valves Diameter Effect on the Efficiency of 3-Inch Hydraulic Ram Pumps, International Journal of Fluid Machinery and Systems 13(3))
